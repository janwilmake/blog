# Talking our way out of reading and writing

We are building computers we can talk to right now. This has been a long time coming, of course, but it's finally getting good with full-duplex voice models like OpenAI's, which can listen and speak at the same time.

It's interesting to zoom out. For about 50 years, we could only read and write to computers. Now we can also speak to them and listen to them: we can talk. Zoom out even further: we started reading and writing roughly 5,000 years ago. Before that, for hundreds of thousands of years, we only communicated by speaking, making sounds, touch and what we could see.

So we've had reading and writing for 5,000 years, and now we have computers that might remove the need for it. They might let us go back to that older way of communicating without losing the advantages reading and writing gave us.

## What reading and writing gave us

Writing brought a lot to the human race that animals never had:

- Remembering things long term
- Sharing big thoughts with big groups of people
- Writing down history
- Drawings and texts that can be interpreted, copied, shared around and moved somewhere else

But a computer that can talk like us can do all of that too. It can remember, it can share, it can carry knowledge across time and place. So why would we still need reading and writing?

## The one advantage I could come up with: silence

The one property I could come up with is that reading and writing can be completely silent. Nobody in the room hears you.

That's cool, but we also have whispering, which is pretty quiet too. And if you really need other people not to hear you, you can just go somewhere else. So that problem is mostly solved already.

What's more interesting is what happens in a room full of people. Put 100 people in a room and they'll talk in groups of four, five, six, or one-on-one. Everybody can focus on the conversation in front of them just fine. But put one person in there behind a screen, reading and writing, and that person suddenly has a much harder time concentrating.

The two modes don't mix well. If 100 people are reading and writing on computers and five of them start talking loudly, everyone on a screen gets distracted. So that's another reason we still need quiet, and therefore reading and writing. Unless everybody starts talking. Then you don't need the quiet anymore, because people who are just talking to each other don't get distracted by other conversations.

There's something about talking. It's much more fluent, and much more what we're built to do as humans, I think.

## A local optimum

So maybe we're in a local optimum right now, where we still need reading and writing.

There are a lot of rough edges with talking to computers. Voice doesn't work well at all yet, especially in noisy environments with multiple speakers. That's not a solved problem. But I think we're close.

When we really get there, we'll have a system that understands its entire surroundings: the people in the room, when it's supposed to speak, when it's supposed to listen, when it's supposed to stay quiet. At that point, maybe we can climb out of the local optimum of reading and writing into a new optimum where we generally don't read and write at all, because I don't really see the advantages anymore.

## The counterargument

One counterargument: some people find it really hard to put things into spoken words, while they find it easy to write them down. Many people find it easier to read about complicated stuff than to talk about it.

My counter to that: maybe that's just because we learned it that way our whole lives. People read a lot about complicated things but rarely talk about them, so of course talking about them feels harder. That might be exactly what keeps us in the local optimum. This is just a theory; there could be other reasons for it.

But if it's true, getting out of this local optimum is much harder than it looks, because it may take generations for people to adapt to only talking again.

## A hundred years

Still, I can imagine it. Maybe it takes decades, maybe a hundred years. But once computers have a truly human-like interface (speaking, listening, making the noises we make), we might go back to that same interface, and stop reading and writing altogether.
